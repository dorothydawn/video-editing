# Brand strategy playbook

This playbook is for creators, coaches and course sellers: personal brands that live on reels, YouTube and a
checkout page.

Evidence tags: **[evidence]** means peer-reviewed work or large datasets. **[practitioner]** means respected
practitioners. **[folklore]** means popular but unsupported. Research notes are from Sept 2026. Items marked
*(recalled)* are standard citations that were not re-opened for these notes.

---

## 1. How brands actually grow (Ehrenberg-Bass Institute)

The main sources are Byron Sharp's *How Brands Grow* (2010) and Romaniuk & Sharp's *How Brands Grow Part 2*
(2016). Both draw on decades of purchase-panel data **[evidence]**.

- **Growth comes from reaching new people.** In the data, small brands have fewer buyers *and* slightly less
  loyal ones (a pattern called "double jeopardy"). So growth comes mostly from more buyers, and in particular
  from light buyers who rarely think about you. For a $27 course, almost every buyer is new and light.
  → Make content for people who don't follow you yet.
- **Mental availability** is the chance that you come to mind in a buying moment. You build it by tying the
  brand to many **category entry points (CEPs)**: the needs, moments and situations that make someone think
  of the category. A vocal coach's CEPs might include:
  - "My voice cracks on high notes"
  - "Karaoke / wedding toast next week"
  - "Choir audition"
  - "Voice tired after teaching"
  - "Want to sing like [artist]"

  Each CEP makes a natural recurring series and a hook.
  (https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/category-entry-points-dissected-how-they-really-contribute-to-growth)
- **Being distinctive (instantly recognisable) matters more than being "meaningfully different"** in Sharp's
  view.
  - Kantar's BrandZ data argues the other side: brands that more people see as *meaningfully different* grow
    faster **[evidence, commercial]**.
  - The working consensus is to do both: be easy to recognise **and** give people a clear reason to choose you.

### Distinctive brand assets (Jenni Romaniuk, *Building Distinctive Brand Assets*, 2018) **[evidence]**

An asset is any brand cue other than the name: a colour, face, font, caption style, sound, catchphrase, set,
or named method. Score each asset on two questions:
- **Fame:** do category buyers link it to you at all?
- **Uniqueness:** does it point *only* to you?

| | Low uniqueness | High uniqueness |
|---|---|---|
| **High fame** | Avoid (it's generic to the category) | **Use or lose** |
| **Low fame** | Test or ignore (every new asset starts here) | **Invest** |

- The **name is still the #1 asset**. Show each new asset next to the name or handle until the asset is
  famous on its own.
- **Consistency is how assets become famous.** Clients and designers get bored of an asset long before the
  audience has even noticed it. Never refresh an asset for novelty's sake.
- **Fluent devices** (System1 / Orlando Wood) are recurring characters or scenarios. System1 cites IPA
  analysis showing campaigns with a fluent device were about 73% more likely to report large profit gains
  **[evidence, commercial]**.
  - **For a creator, the creator's face and voice are the fluent device.** Never hide them, and never open
    with a logo instead of them.

**Studio rule:** give every client 3–5 fixed assets. For example:
- face plus a signature framing or set
- one brand colour
- one caption style
- a sign-off or catchphrase
- a sonic cue (the same SFX palette every time)
- a *named* method

Assets appear in the first 2 seconds.

## 2. Brand building vs sales activation (Binet & Field, IPA)

The source is *The Long and the Short of It* (2013) and its follow-ups, which analyse about 1,000 IPA
effectiveness cases **[evidence]**.

- There are two modes:
  - **Brand building:** broad reach, emotional, pays off over 6+ months.
  - **Activation:** targeted, rational, drives sales now, and its effect decays within weeks.
- The average best split is about **60:40** brand:activation, later revised to about 62:38.
  - Categories people research or buy online need *more* brand building.
  - Small or new brands and direct-response businesses can lean further into activation.
- **Emotional campaigns beat rational ones** on long-term business effects: they are about twice as likely
  to produce top-box profit growth.

**How this maps to creators** **[practitioner inference]**:
- Organic reels and signature series are *brand building*: broad, emotional, useful, entertaining.
- Checkout pages, launch reels, countdowns, testimonial ads and email are *activation*: specific and
  rational, with proof and a call to action.
- Default mix: **60–70% value/brand content, 30–40% offers.**
  - A feed that's all "buy my course" drains trust and reach.
  - A feed with no offers never converts.
- **Activation creative carries the same assets** (face, colour, caption style), so it gets credit for the
  memory the brand content already built.

## 3. Positioning

- **Ries & Trout, *Positioning* (1981) [practitioner, classic].** Own one word or idea in the prospect's
  mind. If you can't be first in a category, create a category you can be first in.
- **April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome* (2019) [practitioner].** Work through these in order:
  1. **Competitive alternatives:** what would the customer do otherwise? For the vocal course: YouTube
     tutorials, a $60/hour private teacher, a choir, or doing nothing.
  2. **Unique attributes** compared with those alternatives.
  3. **Value**, with proof.
  4. **Best-fit customers.**
  5. **Market category.**

  Her follow-up *Sales Pitch* (2023) turns this into a structure that suits sales and checkout pages.
- **Marty Neumeier, *The Brand Gap* / *Zag* [practitioner].** A brand is the customer's gut feeling about
  you.
  - Write an **onlyness statement**: "Our ___ is the only ___ that ___" (who / where / why / when).
    (https://danmall.com/posts/onlyness/)
  - **Competitor-swap test:** put a competitor's name in the statement. If it's still true, it isn't
    positioning.
- **Category design (*Play Bigger*).** The creator-scale version is to name the problem and name the method
  ("tension singing"; "the listening process").
- **Keller's CBBE pyramid and Aaker's brand equity model** are useful audit ladders (awareness → meaning →
  judgement → community), but they are conceptual models, not tested laws.

## 4. Voice and messaging

- **Jobs-to-be-done** (Christensen, *Competing Against Luck*; Bob Moesta's "forces of progress")
  **[practitioner]**. People hire a product to make progress in a particular circumstance. Four forces act
  on the decision:
  - **Push** of the current situation
  - **Pull** of the new solution
  - **Anxiety** about the new solution
  - **Habit** holding them where they are

  For the vocal course, a job might be "sound confident at my sister's wedding in 3 weeks without paying
  for lessons", with anxieties like "I'm tone-deaf" and "it's a scam". The checkout copy should answer those
  anxieties directly.
  - JTBD circumstances are essentially the same thing as CEPs, so the two frameworks connect.
- **StoryBrand (Donald Miller)** **[practitioner; no peer-reviewed validation]**. The customer is the hero
  and the creator is the guide who offers a plan. It's great as a **checklist for sales and checkout
  pages**, but formulaic as a brand voice, and clunky in short form.
- **Message hierarchy:**
  - one core promise
  - three proof pillars
  - a proof bank (numbers, testimonials, credentials)
  - calls to action by funnel stage

  **Each reel carries one pillar**, not all of them.
- **Tone of voice** (the Mailchimp model: "voice doesn't change much, tone changes all the time"):
  - 3–4 traits written as **"X but not Y"**, e.g. "playful but not silly", "expert but not academic".
  - A tone map by context: hook / teaching / testimonial / sales / a student's struggle.
  - A word bank of words to use and avoid.
  - 5 on-voice and 5 off-voice example lines.
- **Naming.** Person's name plus a *named* method or series usually beats an abstract brand name. Names
  should be easy to say and searchable, with the handle and domain free.
- **Sales copy vs graphics.** Graphics should *tease* the benefits in the sales copy's own words. They
  should not reveal the course's internal module names.

## 5. Personal and creator branding

- **Parasocial relationships** (Horton & Wohl, 1956) predict trust in creators and intent to buy from them.
  Credibility works *through* that bond, and feeling "like me" and authenticity strengthen it
  **[evidence, mostly cross-sectional surveys]**.
  - The bond matters most for **low-involvement** buys like a $27 course. High-involvement buys (such as
    $2k coaching) lean more on proof of expertise.
  - Levers that build the bond:
    - talking directly to camera
    - recurring personal details
    - showing the process and failures
    - replying to comments on camera
    - a steady posting rhythm
- **Paddy Galloway** **[practitioner]**:
  - Packaging (title, thumbnail, hook) is the biggest lever.
  - Build repeatable formats.
  - Serve core, casual and new viewers in the same video.
- **Colin & Samir** **[practitioner]**: the winners build *shows*, meaning named, repeatable formats with a
  consistent wrapper.
- **Justin Welsh** **[practitioner]**:
  - One primary platform and a repeatable system.
  - A content matrix: topics × formats.
  - Move the audience onto email.
  - Monetise sparingly.
- **Amanda Natividad, "zero-click content"** **[practitioner]**: deliver the full value inside the post;
  the click is a bonus.

**Studio rule:** every client gets **2–4 signature series**. Each series has:
- a name
- a fixed opening line or card
- a fixed structure
- a fixed look

Vary the topics, never the wrapper.

## 6. Myths to scrub

- **Brand archetypes** (Mark & Pearson; Jungian) are **[folklore]**. They're fine as creative shorthand
  ("Mentor + Jester"), but never present them as science.
  - The evidenced alternative is Jennifer Aaker's (1997) brand personality dimensions: sincerity,
    excitement, competence, sophistication and ruggedness.
- **Colour psychology.**
  - **[evidence]** Labrecque & Milne (2012) found that colour shifts perceived personality (red reads as
    exciting, blue as competent). What mattered for purchase intent was **fit** with the intended
    personality, not a universal emotion.
  - **[folklore]** "90% of snap judgements are based on colour" is a distortion of a 62–90% *range* in
    Singh (2006).
  - **Choose colour for distinctiveness in the category plus personality fit.** Button "wins" in tests come
    from contrast and salience, not the hue itself.

## 7. Checklists

### Brand kit: capture this for every client

**Strategy**
1. Offer ladder (free → low-ticket → high-ticket) and the primary conversion goal.
2. Best-fit audience and their JTBD: circumstance, push, pull, anxieties, habits.
3. Competitive alternatives (including "do nothing" and YouTube) and your unique attributes against them.
4. Positioning statement plus an onlyness statement that passes the competitor-swap test.
5. 5–10 category entry points, which become content series and hooks.
6. Message hierarchy: one core promise, three pillars, and a proof bank (permissioned testimonials, numbers,
   before/after clips).
7. Brand/activation split for the content calendar and the launch calendar.

**Identity (distinctive assets)**

8. Asset inventory:
   - name and handle
   - logo or wordmark
   - 1–2 colours (hex)
   - fonts
   - signature framing and set, wardrobe cues
   - sonic cue or SFX palette
   - catchphrase or sign-off
   - named method

   Tag each asset *keep / invest / drop* using fame × uniqueness.
9. Asset rules: always shown with the name until famous; fixed placement (first 2 seconds, lower third, end
   card); a list of what must never change.
10. Photo library: approved portraits (16:9, square, 4:5, full) and a list of B-roll.

**Voice**

11. 3–4 "X but not Y" traits, a tone map, a word bank, and 5 on-voice and 5 off-voice lines.
12. A hook bank in the client's voice, and calls to action by stage: save/send for brand content, direct
    CTAs for activation.

**Content rules**

13. Signature series: name, opening, structure, length, cadence, platform.
14. Caption preset, text-overlay rules, safe zones, music and SFX licensing, thumbnail template, motion style
    (see `docs/motion.md`).
15. Compliance: testimonial permissions, claims the client can't make, paid-partnership disclosure rules
    (FTC/ASA).

### Brand audit: run at onboarding and every quarter

1. **Recognition test:** hide the name on the last 9 posts. Can a stranger still tell they're one brand?
   Which assets are doing that work?
2. **Consistency count:** how many colours, fonts, caption styles and intros are in use? Target 1–2 of each.
3. **Branding speed:** is the face, name or an asset in the first 2 seconds of every reel?
4. **CEP coverage:** which buying moments are covered and which are missing? Are hooks phrased from the
   viewer's situation?
5. **Positioning clarity:** do the bio and the first line of the sales page say who it's for, what it is,
   and why it beats the alternatives, all within about 5 seconds? Does the onlyness statement survive the
   swap test?
6. **Content mix:** brand content vs offers across the last 30 posts. Is the feed valuable without clicking
   anything?
7. **Voice:** do 10 random captions match the traits? Flag generic "guru" lines.
8. **Funnel continuity:** do reel → bio link → sales page → checkout share the same face, colours, promise
   and words? Does the checkout answer the JTBD anxieties, and include proof and the guarantee?
9. **Proof freshness:** are testimonials recent, specific and permissioned?
10. **Parasocial signals:** direct address, recurring personal elements, replies to comments, posting
    rhythm.
11. **Metrics:**
    - reach to non-followers
    - shares and saves per view
    - follower → email rate
    - checkout conversion
    - an occasional "where have you seen this?" poll to gauge how famous the assets are
